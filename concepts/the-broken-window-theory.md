# The Broken Windows Theory
## Origins
### Historical Background
  The theory was introduced in 1982 by social scientists James Q. Wilson and George L. Kelling in an article published in The Atlantic.
  They studied how visible signs of disorder in urban environments influence human behavior.
  Their core hypotesis was simple but powerful:
    Visible neglect encourages further neglect and eventually serious crime.
### The Classic Broken Window Example
- The theory is often explained using a simple scenario:
  - A building has one broken window
  - If the window is not repaired
    - People assume no one cares
    - More windows get broken
    - Graffiti appears
    - Vandalism increases
    - Eventually, serious crime follows
- Importantly:
  - The broken window itself is not the cause of crime
  - It is a signal of abandonment
  - The signal changes how people behave
### Why this Mattered in Social Scienes
- Before broken windows
  - Policing focused mainly on serious crimes
  - Minor issues were often ignored
- After broken windows
  - Researchers began to study the environmental signals
  - Human behavior was seen as highly contextual
  - Maintenance became a form of prevention
- The environment shapes behavior even without explicit rules
### Why This Transfers So Well to Software
- Software systems are also
  - Human environments
  - Shared spaces
  - Interpreted socially
- Just like neighborhoods
  - Codebases send signals
  - Developers adapt behavior based on what they see
  - Neglect spreads faster than discipline
- A messy system tells developers
  - "No one is watching. Shortcuts are acceptable"

Different domains, same human psycology. 
## Summary
Visible signs of neglect or disorder (like a broken window left unrepaired) signal that no one cares, which encourages more disorder and eventually more serious probrems
